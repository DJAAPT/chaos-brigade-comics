## 6. SAMPLE — Chapters 1–2 (new narrative, transcript-sourced)

### Chapter 1 — An Axe, a Forehead, Four Damage (recap half; sample ends at the inn)

We open split and close together, which for this party counts as character
growth. When we left them: five adventurers marching to the Horned Rabbit with a
noblewoman's rucksack towering over all of them, and a druid across town closing
a deal for forgetfulness with three salt shakers and a map that rewrote itself.
Tonight those two groups become eight — count them: six players, one tag-along,
one owlbear — and by the end of the night they will be seven again, minus one
girl and plus one ransom note, and none of it will be anyone's fault, which is
the most suspicious part.

Lilly, first. She is tagging along now, officially, with the best gear money can
afford and a rucksack twice her size and a tearful butler's blessing still drying
on the doorstep. Pact terms restated at the top of the evening, because Biggins
insists and because he is right: keep her alive, or die trying. No enforcement
mechanism has shown up yet. The magic is patient. So am I. So, unfortunately for
everyone, is the night.

**DM NOTE — HEADCOUNT.** *Eight tonight: six, one, and one bear. I will count
heads in every scene of this journal the way other DMs count arrows. The last
headcount is the whole book. Count with me.*

### Chapter 2 — Doodly Doodly Doo (part 1; sample ends at the butler lore)

The party walks, and Lilly twirls. First time out of her neighborhood in longer
than she can remember — first time out *anywhere*, functionally — and
Neverwinter hits her like weather: dragonborn, elves, orcs, crowds, and a girl
spinning in the street with a pack taller than she is, delighted by all of it.
"Doodly doodly doo," more or less, is the transcript's best rendering of the
sound of a sheltered twenty-two-year-old discovering the world, and I am keeping
it as the chapter title because it is also the sound this whole session makes.

Rocketcandy, walking beside all that joy, asks whether she can carry his axe.
Reader, she has eight hit points. Eight. The paladin is asking a woman with eight
hit points to carry his axe, and Chip — who is not present, who is across town,
who can nevertheless hear axe-related danger the way other druids hear weather —
interjects to object. Then someone does the armor math, and the table goes quiet
in the particular way tables go quiet when the numbers stop making sense: AC
twenty-three. Twenty-three. Higher than the paladins. The girl with eight hit
points is harder to hit than the tank, because her clothes cost more than his
training. She is, as I tell them, a normal human being wearing really great
stuff — take the gear off and she is just another regular human, because not
everyone is a crazy overpowered sorcerer-paladin-warlock tank. The table absorbs
this the way tables absorb all economics: by moving on immediately. But I need
you to remember it, because the whole journal is downstream of one fact: the
most valuable thing in the party is also the most breakable.

Vadania, meanwhile, is doing background checks. How did you meet the butler,
when did your father leave, who raised you — the ranger interrogates the girl's
childhood like it owes him money. Lilly: father gone for years, came back
withdrawn, and Biggins — with the family since before she can remember — brought
him out of it. Barely remembers a time without him. And the stories! Biggins
balances on the backs of chairs to dust. Catches falling things before they fall.
Ruffians flee from him, which Lilly has always assumed is shyness. Vadania asks
whether any of this reads as assassin training, and rolls a ten, and concludes —
on a ten — that she sees it all as perfectly normal Biggins behavior. The thread
stalls there, in the street, because a ten is where threads go to wait. But I
need you to remember that too. Chair backs. Falling objects. Fleeing ruffians.
Normal Biggins behavior.
