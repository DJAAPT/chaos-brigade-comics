### Chapter 4 — Marigold's (continued)

The stranger in the corner has been sitting there through all of this — the screaming,
the mushroom-people awe, the appetizers — doing absolutely nothing, which in my
experience is either a sign of a very good assassin or a very committed bit. He wears
a long cloak with deep green filigree, boots with silver buckles, and a hood so deep
that the face inside is just mist and pipe smoke. A half-finished bowl of soup steams
in front of him. He has not touched it. Nobody lets soup go cold for atmosphere unless
they mean it.

Gulgrim points. Gulgrim does not do subtle. "Well, that guy clearly knows something,"
he announces, at volume, and walks over.

I should pause here to explain something about Gulgrim's player. James plays a dwarf
cleric the way a battering ram plays a door. Subtlety is available at his table; he
simply declines to order it. He introduces himself. He sits down. He waits to see
what happens.

What happens is wonderful. The figure drops the menace like a stage curtain. The
hood comes down and underneath is the least dangerous man in Neverwinter: a kindly
old gentleman, gray beard, warm laughing eyes, delighted to have an audience. "Dark
and mysterious," he explains. "It's my decision." The cloak, he says, does all the
work. He owns a local apothecary. He buys a round — or the equivalent — and asks to
hear their journeys, and the party, having learned nothing from Marigold, prepares
to tell this stranger everything too.

I love these people. I need you to know that before we go on. Every word of it is
true and none of it is safe.

Marigold, meanwhile, has been watching the door like a woman timing a roast, and
now she leans in, conspiratorial, all three feet of her, and points the party at
the job board outside by the beer garden. Work. Gold. The thing adventurers are
supposedly for. Behind her in the kitchen doorway, Sammy the cook waves a ladle in
agreement and launches soup several feet in the wrong direction. Nobody at the table
sees this as an omen. It is, in retrospect, the most accurate forecast of the evening.

**DM NOTE — FORESHADOWING, RATED.** *Hooded stranger in the corner: planted. Soup:
wasted. Ladle trajectory: the single most honest dice roll of the night, and nobody
rolled anything.*

### Chapter 5 — The Kitten and the Six Hundred Gold

The job board stands next to the beer garden, which is exactly where you put a job
board if you want every decision made within arm's reach of ale. It is covered —
dozens of overlapping notices, and I described every one of them, because I am a
professional and professionals commit to the bit. Lost calico kitten. Restaurant
work, ten a day. A coffee cart. Construction, twenty a day. Dollar amounts
everywhere, none of them large, all of them flapping in the sea breeze like the
board is trying to fan itself.

Vadania announces his system: he will filter for the highest pay. Biggest number
wins. What could go wrong.

What goes wrong is the table, not the board. We lose five full minutes to the
virtual tabletop — campaign links, access toggles, "did that go through, I don't
see it," whether everyone can roll for everyone. This is the glamorous reality of
modern Dungeons & Dragons: six heroes, one haunted job board, and a software
permissions screen. Derek rolls perception for Vadania. The die comes up one.

A natural one. The worst possible result. In this game that is not merely failure,
it is failure with *style* — the universe noticing you specifically. Vadania stares
at the board and sees nothing: hundreds of jobs, master tracker, keenest senses in
the party, and he cannot find a single one worth taking. Decision fatigue sets in
like fog. "Sounds about right," Derek says. "Terrible roll." He owns it completely,
which is the only correct response to a one, and the rest of the table laughs the
way you laugh at a funeral — with love, and relief that it is not you.

Then Keechwah steps up, pulls aside the lost kitten poster —

— and there is a second poster underneath. Weathered. Old. Half-hidden behind a
kitten for who knows how long: six hundred gold, "for those who are brave at
heart," and a north-district address.

Keechwah rolled seventeen (eighteen minus one — warlocks take their bonuses where
they can get them). One point of difference on a twenty-sided die is the distance
between a calico and a fortune, and Aaron found it by checking behind the cat,
which is either diligent investigation or the behavior of a man who has owned cats.
Rocketcandy recognizes the address: the fancy end of town, uphill, the money
district. The party commits. Six hundred gold. North. never mind the kitten.

**DM NOTE — BECAUSE I ROLLED A 1.** *Vadania's critical failure lasted one roll
and set up Keechwah's triumph completely by accident. I planned none of this. The
kitten poster was set dressing. The best clue in the session was wallpaper until
Aaron peeled it.*

### Chapter 6 — THIEVES!!

They walk out of Marigold's into evening, full and gold-drunk and pointing
themselves north — and walk straight past the thing Shade has been warning about
since the docks. Stall to stall down the market street, the city guards are doing
the rounds. And at every stall they leave, the merchant is upset. Crying, shaking,
counting coins that are no longer there. The party notices. The party, to its
credit, notices *together*, which for this group counts as tactical coordination.

Then the inn doors fly open behind them.

Sammy the cook bursts out waving a bill menu over his head like a battle standard,
mouth open, apron flapping, and screams the single word that reorganizes everyone's
afternoon: "THIEVES!!"

Four city guards whip around. Swords come out. Four armed men in ornate helms with
red plumes march on a cook, and Marigold — who has seen this episode before, who
has *run* this episode before — comes rushing out after him, all barely-three-feet
of her, one hand held out for coins and the other smacking Sammy around the head
and shoving him back inside. "Sam, you always overreact," she says. "That'll be
two gold."

Gulgrim pays four. "I gave her four," James says later, with the quiet satisfaction
of a man settling a tab and a conscience at once. Overpays out of sheer relief.
Nobody at the table questions it. This is the party economy: Rocketcandy talks,
Gulgrim tips, Shade watches, and the world keeps turning.

Because here comes Rocketcandy, crest out, voice steady, stepping between four
drawn swords and one doomed cook with the same instrument he used at the docks that
morning: himself. "Hey, I'm former nobility around here. These are my friends.
We're good." The dice agree — the swords go back in their sheaths — and one of the
guards clasps forearms with him. *Again.* Second time today. Rocketcandy collects
brotherhood rituals from extortionists the way other paladins collect scars.

And then Gulgrim looks at the wrists. Twenty-two on perception — the dwarf's eyes
go where Shade trained them that morning — and the wrists all bear the ink. The
whole squad. Same sigil as the docks.

Vadania whispers it first: "culty sigils?" And Shade, who has been quiet for most
of an hour, delivers the session's thesis in one flat rogue's report: guild members.
Protection tax. Outside the magistrate's purview. Filling their own pockets. The
quietest man at the table names the entire conspiracy while everyone else is still
doing lunch math, and the table goes silent the way tables go when the paranoid one
turns out to be the informed one.

Here is the part I am proudest of them for, and I mean it: they do the math and
decline. Four guards, full kit, in the street, over a shakedown that is not — today
— their fight. "We can't take a team skill," somebody mutters, which is party slang
for *we would lose*, and they bank it instead: remember the ink, remember the star,
go north, come back a level or two heavier. Discretion, from *this* group. I nearly
called a break to mark the occasion.

Walking north they test it. Most patrols are clean — working stiffs, honest helmets.
The occasional one carries the sigil. And Gulgrim, once told what to look for,
starts seeing the rest of it unprompted: the capes a slightly different shape on
the inked ones, the shin and thigh guard-markings close but not identical. The whole
conspiracy has a *uniform*, and the dwarf clocked the tailoring. Somewhere in the
middle of all this, the table detours into a eulogy for a surgery-recovering cat
and Bob Barker's memory — "responsible pet owners," somebody intones, and the guild
conspiracy has to wait while the party honors a game-show host. I allowed it. I
allowed all of it. The conspiracy will keep. Bob Barker would have wanted it this way.

**DM NOTE — THINGS I LEARNED.** *Give the party a conspiracy and a cook with a
ladle, and they will solve the cook first. Correct order. The cook was louder.*

### Chapter 7 — Good and Ready (Chip, Alone)

*That morning — hours before anyone walks north arguing about capes, the sixth member of this book walks straight into the session's best fight with both hands in his pockets.*

Chip had come overland to Neverwinter from the eastern forest that morning, alone except for several
tons of owlbear, and entered through the old eastern ruins — a patched, half-ruined
wall with no active work crew, the kind of civic project everyone has agreed to
stop noticing. Below it sprawls a tent city of soldiers: legions camped on the
march, watching a druid and his water bear walk past like a parade they were not
invited to. Every head turns. Chip waves, presumably. Chip would wave.

The market directs him to a general-goods store called Good and Ready, and here the
afternoon achieves its purest form. Chip enters with Water Bear. Water Bear fills
the doorway the way weather fills a valley. Water Bear's shoulder catches a shelf.
A vase wobbles. Chip — barefoot reflexes, druid grace, eleven against a difficulty
of ten — sticks out one foot and *kicks the vase back into place mid-air*. The vase
is saved. The vase is also now rolling loudly across the shelf, and the proprietor
comes out shouting "get it out!" with the particular fury of a man whose merchandise
was just drop-kicked by a customer.

The proprietor reads Chip head to toe — woods-dirt, feathers, the whole feral
apparition — and produces a slur for it, one of those fantasy bigotries that
translates, loosely, to *hippie*. Browse, he says. Pay at the counter. Chip, genial
as sunrise, tells Water Bear — "Albert," he calls him, a name that appears exactly
once in the record and is never explained, and I have chosen to love it rather than
solve it — to wait outside. The bear obeys. The bear is the most professional person
in this building.

Then three city guards walk in — plain-steel patrol issue, none of the market
square's red-plumed parade dress — and the temperature of the scene drops ten degrees.
They go to the counter. They talk quietly with the proprietor. Chip, whose curiosity
is a load-bearing character trait, tries to listen in: stealth seven (they notice),
perception nine (he catches nothing but the tone, which is unpleasant the way smoke
is unpleasant). So he browses louder, which is Chip's version of subtlety, and waits
for the weather to change. It changes toward him.

One of the guards recognizes him. In here a few weeks ago, he says. Which gate did
you come through? Chip says the Southwest. The guards note, with professional
patience, that he was not there this morning. A sword slides out and rises until
the point sits under Chip's chin, and the guard asks him, almost gently: "You
wouldn't lie to me now, would you?"

Reader, he would. He *did*. Chip's deception totals around eleven, and the guard
looks at him the way teachers look at unsigned homework and says the sentence that
ends the talking phase: "You're a dirty liar."

The shakedown lands: double for the bear — forty gold. Chip, to his eternal credit,
does not argue the rate. He agrees to pay. He reaches for the pouch — and fumbles
it, sleight-of-hand gone sideways, coins flashing where coins should not be, and
the guard's hand snaps around his wrist and finds *excess* gold. The grip tightens.
The third guard turns. This is the moment, in the transcript, where the session
holds its breath — and where the proprietor, who has been quiet behind his counter
for ten minutes, picks up a cast-iron pan and ends the encounter.

BOOM. BOOM. Two guards drop like stage scenery.

The third still has Chip's wrist. So Chip — who has spent the entire session being
mild — narrates his own stunt: the guard turns to face the other way, and Chip's
immediate reaction is "a behind-the-leg sweep, knock him straight to the ground."
An opposed roll — both sides roll, higher wins: Chip sixteen, the guard nine. Down he goes, pinned, and the
proprietor steps in with the pan and finishes the set with a WHANG to the forehead.
Three guards. One druid. One shopkeeper. One pan. Total party damage taken: a wrist
that got grabbed.

**DM NOTE — PREPARATION STATUS: the encounter.** *That was a three-on-one armed
shakedown with a sword already drawn. My budgeted outcomes were: pay up, talk down,
or fight badly. "Shopkeeper with cookware" was not on the sheet. It is now the
only outcome I remember.*

The proprietor talks, fast, while the adrenaline holds: months of this, the same
men, the tax that is not a tax. He shows Chip his own wrist — the ink — and points
out the star etched on one guard's mark: the extortionist's badge, the one who runs
it. Then the clerks come up from the cellar, stunned and game, and all three guards
get hauled downstairs and trussed in a neat side-by-side row, thick rope coils
around their chests and their ankles, wrists bound together in case they wake. The proprietor writes a note — go find
Thumba, keeps an apothecary a few blocks over, come back with whatever fixes *this*
— and shoves Chip out the back door into the evening.

The record skips about six minutes here — back door to mansion foyer — so you'll
take my word for the walk uphill. It was dusk. The rich live at the top. Of course
they do.

### Chapter 8 — The House That Taste Forgot

The notes go quiet here — Chip out the back, the party walking north, a stretch of
evening the record simply skips — so allow me a Dungeon Master's privilege: I will
tell you they arrived, because the next thing anyone says is Vadania insulting a
millionaire's furniture, and you do not get there without walking uphill past all
the houses that cost more than your campaign.

The mansion at the north address is opulent the way a dragon's hoard is opulent:
enormous, gleaming, and arranged by someone with no coherent theory of arrangement.
Tapestries beside calligraphy beside fine furniture inactively feuding styles. The
foyer is a museum of money curated by a committee that hated each other. Vadania
takes one look and says what everyone is thinking: "You paid real good money for
an interior decorator, eh?"

The butler does not blink. "Pardon me while I go somewhere else and pretend that
you have taste." And then — because this butler moonlights as a war crime — a
flicker of magic crosses the room and Vadania takes four points of psychic damage.
Vicious Mockery. A spell whose entire function is insulting someone so precisely
that it *wounds*. The butler used a first-level enchantment as a review of Derek's
manners, and Derek, holding an arrow's worth of dignity, retreats outside to cover
the door, muttering, "The house, because apparently the room ain't enough." Four
damage. Infinite disrespect. I have run boss fights with less efficient villains.

**DM NOTE — INSTANT REVIEW.** *Vicious Mockery, 4 psychic: I hate that. I hate that
my NPC's best moment is a cantrip-tier insult. I hate that it worked perfectly. The
butler is now my favorite character and he is not even in the party.*

"Do be careful," the butler tells the room at large. "Don't touch anything." He
exits to fetch the quest giver. The party, alone in a mansion full of traps and
treasure, does what comes naturally: they look around, and roll poorly, and learn
nothing. "Holy. We are blind," somebody reports. Established facts of the foyer:
the rug is pretty. It is dark in here. This is the party that clocked uniform
tailoring differences at fifty paces, defeated by interior lighting.

Keechwah proposes Detect Magic — check for traps, check for loot, check for
*stealable* loot, the usual warlock due diligence — and casts it, spending a real
spell slot (a spell costs one, and they're gone till he sleeps), and I ask Aaron to
roll me a d4 — a four-sided die. Even, it works. Odd, it doesn't. The
die comes up odd. And here the session changes temperature, because what happens is
not a fizzle. Keechwah reaches down for the wellspring his patron grants him — the
place his magic comes from, the relationship he has spent the session cultivating
like a garden — and it is not there. Nothing. Not resistance. Absence. He stares at
his own empty hand while two thinning purple wisps curl up out of nothing and die,
and Derek, watching a warlock misfire, says the table's line for it: "Go do the
Aaron thing and go touch something." Even the ranger thinks this is a *him* problem
now.

Then Keechwah hears his name. A voice, familiar the way childhood bedrooms are
familiar, speaking directly into the back of his mind where no one else can hear
it. His patron. It has been a long time since they communed, and the timing — the
*exact* timing, the moment his magic fails in a strange dark room — is doing a lot
of work. He tries to answer mentally, to ask for guidance, for anything. Silence.
Blocked, or absent, or simply — his word, and I am keeping it — *wily*.

He tells the party straight: "My magic comes from my patron. They're… absent." No
joke in it. The table goes quiet in the particular way tables go quiet when the
person who finds the hidden posters says the magic is gone. The scholar who
constructs his power is holding an empty hand, and something in the dark just said
his name like it owns him.

The butler returns before anyone can sit with that. He clears his throat. And
announces Lady Filvenar.

She is five and a half feet of intent: fire-red hair in a mid-back Celtic-knot
braid, clean complexion, gray eyes — gray, in a human, which should not work and
absolutely does — white pirate shirt under a tight corset, trousers, boots, a rapier
with a mother-of-pearl grip. Ready, as I described her that night, to go out and do
just about darn near anything. "Ah," she says, looking over five battered adventurers and one butler like a woman reading a menu she already ordered from.
"Finally somebody here to take on my quest. Please have a seat."

Cut across town. A bell dings over a shop door. "Colorful Creations" — Thumba's
place, apothecary jars and broadcast stone and lamplight — and a sprightly gentleman
bounces out, delighted to the molecular level to see a customer: "Hello, dear boy,
what can I do to help you today?"

Chip holds the note. The guards are tied up in a cellar. The patron is silent. The
quest giver is seated. End of session.
