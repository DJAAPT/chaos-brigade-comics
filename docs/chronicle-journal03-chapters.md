### Chapter 3 — Mr. Water Bear Owl

Round the last corner and there they are: a block or two ahead, going the same
direction, unmistakable at any distance. Water Bear first — you always see the
bear first, it is most of the view — and behind him, Chip, exactly where a druid
should be, which is wherever the bear isn't looking. Rocketcandy does not wait
for consensus. He runs up the street and scratches the owlbear under the beak,
and the party falls in lockstep around the two of them like this was the plan
all along, and the plan, for once, is exactly this. Stories get swapped at
walking pace: job board, six hundred gold, a girl with a rucksack and a dream.
Eight heads now. Count them. Six, one, and one bear.

Chip clocks it first — perception thirteen, last-second save. Lilly, captivated,
is reaching out to pet the Water Bear. So Chip does what druids do: speaks a word
in Druidic, and the bear steps back, feathers raised, overwhelmed by the city and
the new smells and the small human coming at him like weather. Lilly, undeterred,
attempts Animal Handling and rolls a six. A six, reader, is how you fail at
petting. She rushes him like it's chase-play. Chip calmly puts himself between
them — barricade of one druid — and delivers the ruling that will govern this
relationship going forward: "You have to ask the bear." So she does. Hands
together, formal as court: "Mr. Water Bear Owl, can I pet you?" Then, for good
measure, she mimics the Druidic back at him, syllables and all, and touches —
one touch, tolerated, not enjoyed — and Chip calls it. Enough. Fish later, he
promises the bear, out loud, in front of everyone, and the bear accepts the
terms, and Lilly learns her first lesson in adventuring: the wild has office
hours, and they are Chip's.

**DM NOTE — DIPLOMACY.** *She asked the bear. Nobody told her to ask the bear.
The 6 became a negotiation, the negotiation became a fish debt, and the fish debt
is now canon. This is how the best rules get written: by accident, out loud,
where I can hear them.*

### Chapter 4 — Battle of the Bards

The crowds thicken as evening comes on, and then the street opens up and there
it is: lanterns, tables, barmaids with trays held high, a band on a stage, and
over all of it a giant sign reading BATTLE OF THE BARDS. A festival that happens
to have a quest in it, or a quest that happens to have a festival in it — either
way the party walks in with eight heads total, beak included, and starts casing the joint
like it owes them money. Chip and Rocketcandy clock it first, together, the two
highest perceptions at the table arriving at the same conclusion from opposite
directions: people carrying instruments. People *strumming*. Somewhere in this
crowd is a moody band, and Chip has a stone that needs gloom.

Logistics first, because Gulgrim insists and because Gulgrim is right: count the
party (eight, bear included), find the innkeeper, demand three rooms. The
Horned Rabbit's bartender looks up — "what'll it be, short stuff?" — and quotes
the night: one room left, sleeps four in two beds, a hundred gold plus drinks.
Gulgrim does the math the way other men draw swords. "Never mind," he says, and
walks away, and reports to the party that they will not be sleeping here. A
hundred gold. For four beds. The man who paid double for lunch on principle
would not pay quadruple for a mattress, and honestly? Correct call. The night is
young, the festival is free, and beds are for parties without a broadcast stone
and a ransom note in their future. (He doesn't know about the note yet. Nobody
knows about the note yet. The note is the last page of this journal and I am
enjoying every step toward it.)

Vadania finds the mark the way Vadania finds everything: by staring until the
world cooperates. Twenty-three on perception, and there they are, second or third
in line: dark leathers, browns, blacks, low jubilant energy in a field of
celebration — the moodiest band at the festival, gloomy on purpose, perfect.
Rocketcandy spots the apparatus around them (ten on perception, enough): a
clipboard, a rope, a bouncer logging instrument groups into the band area. Gated
community. Time to knock.

**DM NOTE — PROCUREMENT.** *One room, four beds, a hundred gold. Declined. My
party will fight a dragon for free and unionize against a mattress. I run the
game; I don't run the economy. The economy is James.*

### Chapter 5 — The Axe Trick

What Rocketcandy does next deserves precision, because my players will tell this
story wrong for years. He does not ask permission. He does not check the lineup.
He walks up to the roped-off band area with an axe and a grin and performs:
spin, catch, jump-rope *with the axe*, behind the back, limbo under the spinning
handle while the stage bard — a professional, mid-set, derailed in real time —
starts vamping along behind him, scatting nonsense to keep up. Dirty twenty on
performance — a natural twenty with bonuses stacked on top, the best roll the game
allows. The crowd comes to *us*. The moody band notices. Auggie from the bar
notices. The whole festival tilts ten degrees toward the paladin, and for one
shining minute the plan is working exactly as no one planned it.

Then — because this table cannot leave perfection alone — he tries a
back-handspring. In full plate. Acrobatics two. He goes over like a felled keep,
face-first into the flagstones, helmet somewhere behind him, and the dexterity save comes up seven, which means
he fails *at* falling, and the axe comes down butt-first onto his own forehead
for four bludgeoning damage, to enormous laughter. Byodan's assessment from the
sidelines: "impressive part is he had the room to do this." Aaron, helpfully:
"Dead bodies and blood everywhere." Derek, holding the only sane position left:
looks at Rocky and asks what any of it was for. Answer: the band. It worked. The
band noticed, the crowd laughed *with* the plan rather than at it, and Rocketcandy
buys the-dark-and-mysterious three drinks with one hand while holding the other
hand to his head for the rest of the evening — rolling perception at disadvantage
from then on (roll twice, take the worse), wandering back to the group having
noticed nothing, which was accurate, because there was nothing to notice except
him.

The band, introduced at last: Lom, no instrument, singer, frontman of the dark
and mysterious, playing their eighth Battle of the Bards "this cycle of soul."
They will talk custom songs after the set, over drinks, stage-side after the
applause. Terms to follow. Pride currently intact. Foreheads, various.

**DM NOTE — STUNT ACCOUNTING.** *Dirty 20: crowd, band, plan. Acrobatics 2: floor.
DEX 7: forehead. Net: positive. I have stopped costing out my players' dignity
because the exchange rate favors them.*

### Chapter 6 — Lilly, Lost and Found

Keechwah has one job: watch the girl. He claims the Lilly-watch like a man
accepting a sacred trust, and then — his words, on the record — he looks away
*for one second* to follow a barmaid's tray across the room, rolls a seven, and
loses her completely. One second. Seven. Gone. The babysitter has been
babysat by the room, and the room is very good at it. The table erupts the way
tables erupt when the careful one fails carefully, and the search begins.

Shade finds her. Of course Shade finds her. Fifteen on perception, slipped
through the crowd like it was weather, and there she is across the way at a
table with a great view of the stage: pack down, drink in hand, alone, delighted.
Age check first — Chip does the math out loud, Wisdom twenty-one: early twenties,
twenty-two, twenty-three, old enough to drink, crisis of propriety averted — and
then the confrontation. The drink came from a "pretty lady" barmaid with ten
drinks on a tray and a large coin purse. The table, the barmaid says, "just
opened." Lilly gulps the frothy ale, spills it down her corset, and then — in
the move that stops three conversations at once — slaps her satchel onto the
table, and it spills *hundreds* of gold. "Gave her one of these," somebody says,
meaning the purse, meaning someone handed this girl hundreds of gold and no
instructions. Heads whip around the room. Shade's whole Thieves-Guild sense goes
off like a bell. Vadania's verdict is instant: pocket it. She cinches it shut.
Vadania rolls fifteen for watchers: no overt move — the group looks too hardened
to rob, tonight, in this light. Tonight. In this light. Qualifiers doing heavy
lifting.

Then the lecture, and it is a good one, and it does not work. Stay with someone.
Hold the table. Don't wander. Lilly hears the concern, appreciates the concern,
and responds by wanting new friends and meeting people, and flashes the Gulgrim
dagger *and* the mother-of-pearl rapier for emphasis. Vadania grabs for the
rapier's elbow — athletics four against five, the die's little joke — and the
blade comes all the way out, fully drawn, over ale, in a crowd, held by a girl
who has owned it for hours. Only the ring of party bodies around her keeps it
from becoming an incident. The plan, revised under pressure: leave her at the
table — "our table" now, claimed by presence — signal Keechwah to sit on her,
and the rest go back to Chip and the bear and wait out the band's set. The
babysitter, chastened, sits. The table holds. The set plays on.

### Chapter 7 — The Chaos Brigade (Band Edition)

The dark and mysterious take the stage, and I want the record to show what that
means: songs about darkness and undead and graveyards and dead puppies, a
showpiece ballad about salt versus sugar that took, by their own account, three
weeks to write. The crowd receives it the way crowds receive homework. Scattered
cheers. Cold tables. Our party applauds strategically — loudly, visibly, from
exactly the right seats — because Chip needs these men agreeable and agreeable
is a currency you mint yourself.

Meanwhile Chip has solved the bear problem the unglamorous way: the valet flips
the book — "what do we charge for an owlbear?" — and lands on one gold a night,
horses and donkeys giving the paddock a wide berth on arrival. Chip pays. Gets
the ticket. Heads back to the table, where the evening has settled into its
rhythm: Rocketcandy with two tankards at one gold each (one for drinking, one
pressed to the forehead), Shade scanning the room in active hope of *not* seeing
the bard he drunkenly told their whole story to last time, Vadania watching
everything the way Vadania watches everything now, which is constantly and
without blinking.

Post-set, glasses up, Chip buys the band three drinks and pitches it: a hooded
figure, walking at night, needs an ominous aura in song form — custom work,
magic involved, favor owed to a friend. The band confers. Three weeks for salt
and sugar, remember. Chip watches their faces the way Chip watches everything,
which is better than anyone credits, and catches it: the face-scratch. Dirty
twenty on perception. The tell lands, the demand follows: four thousand gold,
discounted to thirty-eight hundred, for one custom song. Chip looks at them the
way druids look at clear-cuts. "Unreasonable," he says. "When's the last time you
were paid that much for a song?" The table holds its breath. The band holds its
price. And then, from the next table over, a bearded minstrel with a loot starts
laughing — gutturally, helplessly, the laugh of a man watching amateurs get
skinned — and stands up and runs them off his stage: first Battle of the Bards,
not eighth, not second, quit trying to swindle, *beat it*. Lom's crew scatters.
And the bearded man turns to our table, loot in hand, and introduces himself as
Bearnuts.

Yes. *Bearnuts.* That is his name. That is also James's name — Bearnuts [James],
dwarf cleric, metronome, poop-in-a-hole.jpeg. No, the
table never resolves it. Yes, I have chosen to love it rather than solve it, the
same way I loved Albert. Moving on.

Bearnuts — the minstrel, the new one, the one with the loot and the beard and
the fine clothes — heard the whole pitch and makes a counter-offer with no gold
in it at all. He is ex-Wandering Minstrels. He needs a backup band for a
slam-bang set. We back him, he writes the song by morning. Trade, not
transaction. And just like that the evening inverts: auditions over, band formed.
Keechwah on lute (he has one, random Compass Rose pickup, cannot play it, bulk
complaints noted and overruled). Gulgrim on block-and-stick, keeping time like a
metronome with a grudge. Shade on bagpipes — volunteered sight unseen, which
tells you everything about David's confidence and nothing about his musicality.
Rocketcandy on fiddle. Vadania on fife. Chip on backup and vibes, with one firm
condition: no animal act. Lilly is designated crowd groupie and woo-girl, a title
she accepts like a knighthood. Bardic Inspiration d6s all around — borrowed luck,
a six-sided bonus for everyone — Guidance d4 on top — a little druid encouragement —
and the band takes its name the only way it could: the Chaos Brigade.
Huddle speech — music from the heart — hands in. Even the bear would have joined
if the bear had hands, which, no animal act, fine.

The set, because you are wondering and because the transcript kept receipts:
Chip's Druidcraft — a free baby spell, this one for weather and candlelight —
snuffs every third candle and puts a breeze in everyone's hair.
The minstrel announces the Chaos Brigade. They play. Keechwah adds Thaumaturgy
— same weight class, this one for tremors — under the bass so the ground itself
keeps time.
tremors under the bass so the ground itself keeps time. Scores come in scattered
— thirteen here, sixteen there, a twenty from Keechwah (twelve plus four plus
four, rerolled fourteen with advantage and kept honest), an eleven from Shade, a
six from Vadania (three plus one plus two, and no, I don't know what the two was
either), a seventeen from Rocketcandy — and it averages to fourteen. DC was
twelve — Difficulty Class, the number to beat. Fifteen would have been frenzy, twenty would have been Beatles. Fourteen
is: competent, joyful, standing ovation, ushered off before anyone asks for an
encore. Good. Not Beatles. The distinction matters to history.

**DM NOTE — AVERAGING.** *Thirteen, sixteen, twenty, eleven, six, seventeen.
Average: fourteen. Passes twelve, misses fifteen, never sniffs twenty. The
stenography of this set took longer than the set. Fourteen forever. Good, not
Beatles.*

### Chapter 8 — Meet Me South of Town

After the ovation, business: Chip repeats the pitch, hands over Thumba's stone,
and the minstrel promises a mystery banger by morning — meet in front of the
Horned Rabbit, handshake, he disappears inside with our apothecary's favor in
his pocket and the whole table watching him go. Then table talk, banter, the
room settling — and Gulgrim stands to reclaim their table, walks over, and stops.

Lilly is gone. Pack gone. Table empty but for one thing: his own gifted dagger,
driven point-first through a folded note. Ben reads it aloud, and the room — the
real room, my players, out of character for the first time all night — goes
completely silent: "meet me on the road south of town if you want your little
adventurer back."

Lowercase. No signature. Pinned with *his* knife. Whoever took her was close
enough to touch her table, quiet enough that a festival never noticed, and
polite enough to use Gulgrim's own steel as the envelope. The pact says keep her
alive or die trying. The pact does not say anything about ransom notes, because
when Gulgrim shook Biggins' forearm, nobody at the table — *nobody*, myself
included — had priced this outcome. That is the session. That is the book. Seven
heads. Count them. Seven.

**DM NOTE — FINAL HEADCOUNT.** *Eight came in. Seven walk out. The arithmetic of
this journal is one girl, one dagger, one road south. See you there.*
